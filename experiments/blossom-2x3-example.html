Why tile swapping needs Blossom: a complete 2×3 worked example

Every number on this page was produced by the solver itself on a real board — nothing here is illustrative.

The one-sentence version

Improving the board by swapping pairs of tiles is a maximum-weight matching problem. The graph you match on is the complement of the grid — two tiles may be swapped only if they are not neighbours. On a 2×3 window that complement graph already contains two triangles, so it is not bipartite, and the Hungarian algorithm (which requires a bipartite graph) cannot be used at all. Blossom can, because matching in a general graph is exactly what it does.

1. The move: swap two tiles, re-spin them independently

Take two cells u and v. Exchange the pieces in them, and choose a fresh rotation for each piece independently. Surrounding tiles never move.

Each tile shows its four edge colours as wedges (top = North, right = East, bottom = South, left = West), plus the piece id and rotation. An internal edge is green when the two facing colours agree and red when they clash. The objective counts 2 for every clashing internal edge (each such edge is seen from both endpoints) and 8 for every non-grey border edge.

Figure 1 — the original window. A 2×3 block taken from a real 16×16 board, sitting in its frozen surround (pale band). All 7 internal edges clash; 0/7 are matched.

2. Which pairs are legal? Not the neighbours

The change in objective caused by swapping u and v is a sum of terms, one per side of u and one per side of v. Each term compares the new colour on that side against the colour it must match — and that required colour comes from the tile on the other side.

That is exactly why neighbours are forbidden. If u and v share an edge, then the colour u must match on that side is the content of v — which is itself moving. The term is no longer a constant, the two cells stop being independent, and the swap's value can no longer be written down in advance.

So the legal pairs are precisely the pairs of cells that are not neighbours.

Figure 2a — the grid graph. 7 edges: the pairs that are forbidden.
Figure 2b — the swap graph. 8 edges: the pairs that are allowed, each labelled with its gain. It is the complement of Figure 2a.

Six cells have 15 pairs. 7 are grid edges, so 15 − 7 = 8 survive into the swap graph. Every one of them carries a genuine gain, computed by trying all 16 rotation combinations and keeping the best:

#pairgainnote
1a–c6best single swap in the whole window
2a–f4corner to far corner
3b–f4top-middle to bottom-right
4a–e2diagonal
5b–d2diagonal
6c–d2diagonal
7c–e2diagonal
8d–f2bottom row, distance two

Gain = the reduction in objective; a negative delta is a positive gain. Pairs with zero gain would simply be omitted as edges.

3. The swap graph is not bipartite — and that is the whole point

Take the three cells a, c and e:

  • a and c are in the same row but two columns apart → not neighbours → edge a–c exists.
  • c and e are diagonal → not neighbours → edge c–e exists.
  • a and e are diagonal → not neighbours → edge a–e exists.

All three edges are present, so {a, c, e} is a triangle — an odd cycle. The solver confirms there are exactly two: {a, c, e} and {b, d, f}.

Figure 3 — the triangle {a, c, e} (red). Its mirror {b, d, f} is the other one.

A graph is bipartite if and only if it has no odd cycle. A triangle is an odd cycle of length 3, so the swap graph is not bipartite, and the solver's 2-colouring attempt fails, as it must.

Worth noting: 2×3 is the smallest grid where this happens. A 1×4 grid complement is a 3-edge path and a 2×2 complement is two disjoint edges — both bipartite. At 2×3 the triangles appear.

4. Why Hungarian cannot be used

The Hungarian algorithm solves assignment: put objects on one side, slots on the other, and match across. It needs the two sides to be genuinely different sets, with every edge crossing between them. Our problem has one set of six cells and edges drawn between members of that same set. There is no way to split {a, c, e} into two sides when each pair among them is an edge — try it and you are forced to put two adjacent vertices on the same side.

This is not a technicality that can be worked around. It is the difference between two different polytopes:

  • Bipartite matching is described completely by the degree constraints Σe ∈ δ(v) xe = 1. That is why Hungarian, and the linear program behind it, is exact there.
  • General matching needs extra constraints that bipartite matching does not have: Edmonds' odd-set (blossom) inequalities Σe ∈ E[S] xe ≤ (|S|−1)/2 for every odd set S.

Here is the concrete damage, run through the actual solver. Give the triangle {a, c, e} three edges of weight 1 each and everything else weight 0:

triangle {a,c,e} all weights 1: max weight matching = 1
  (LP without the odd-set inequality would give 1.5;
   the blossom constraint x(E[S]) <= 1 is exactly what cuts that off)

The straight LP relaxation happily sets all three triangle edges to ½, reaching 1.5 — an answer no real matching can achieve, because a matching can take at most 1 edge out of any triangle. The missing constraint is xac+xce+xae ≤ 1, i.e. Edmonds' odd-set inequality with S = {a, c, e}. Shrinking such odd sets into a single super-vertex is literally what "blossom" names, and it is the machinery Blossom VI brings and Hungarian does not have.

5. Running the maximum-weight matching

We ask for the maximum-weight matching of the 8-edge graph in Figure 2b. Blossom VI returns:

--- maximum weight matching (Blossom) ---
total weight 10, 2 pairs:
  a-c
  b-f

Because this graph has only six vertices, we can also enumerate all possible matchings by hand and check the answer. There are 25 of them:

brute-force MAX WEIGHT MATCHING = 10 : a-c b-f
Blossom says                      10 : a-c b-f   -> AGREE

So Blossom's answer is provably optimal here, and it is an answer that picks up two swaps at once — worth 10, versus 6 for the best single swap. That is the gain from doing the matching properly instead of repeatedly taking the best remaining pair.

6. One more wrinkle: the pairs must not touch each other

There is a second, subtler use of the same "don't touch" idea. We banned the two cells within a pair from being neighbours. But if we apply two swaps at once, say a–c and b–f, then a and b are neighbours across the two pairs — and we are right back in the same trap, because each one's required colour is the other's content and both are moving.

The fix is to require an induced matching: no cell of one pair may neighbour a cell of another. The solver checks it directly. Applying both of Blossom's pairs anyway gives:

[check] applying Blossom's 2 pairs WITHOUT the filter:
         predicted 10, measured 6  -> NOT additive

Read that line carefully — it is the reason a matching solver alone is not the end of the story. The two individual gains were 6 and 4, so the naive prediction was 10. The board only improved by 6, because the shared edge a–b made the two swaps interfere. The sum of parts is not the whole.

So after the matching, the operator greedily walks the matched pairs in decreasing order of gain and keeps a pair only if it touches no already-kept pair:

--- greedy filter (additivity requires an INDUCED matching) ---
  keep a-c (gain 6)
  drop b-f (touches an already chosen pair)
  drop b-d (touches an already chosen pair)
  drop d-f (touches an already chosen pair)

Enumerating all 25 matchings confirms the filter lost nothing on this instance: 9 of the 25 are induced, and the best induced matching is worth 6 — exactly the a–c the greedy filter kept.

brute-force MAX WEIGHT INDUCED MATCH = 6 : a-c

On a 2×3 window an induced matching can never hold more than one pair, which is why this example lands on a single swap. On larger windows several survive, and that is where the matching earns its keep. Choosing a maximum-weight induced matching is NP-hard in general, so Blossom's ordinary matching plus this filter is a practical compromise, not an exact solver for the induced problem.

7. Applying the swap

Swap a and c, with the rotations the search chose (piece 122 was at rotation 3, piece 200 at rotation 2; each keeps its own rotation, so the two tiles simply trade places):

swap a<->c : piece 122 rot 3  <->  piece 200 rot 2
Before. 0/7 internal edges matched.
After. 1/7 internal edges matched.

Cell a now shows colours 21, 8, 14, 16 where it needs 21, 20, 15, 16 — its North edge is now satisfied. Cell c shows 20, 14, 19, 10 where it needs 19, 11, 16, 10 — its West edge is now satisfied.

predicted gain 6, measured gain 6, cost 878 -> 872

Predicted equals measured, exactly. That is the payoff for all the care about which pairs are legal: the gain we computed from the matching is the gain the board actually delivers, to the unit.

8. Summary

  1. The move is: swap two tiles, re-spin each independently.
  2. Two tiles may be swapped only if they are not neighbours, otherwise the swap's value cannot be written down in advance. Hence the graph is the complement of the grid.
  3. Picking the best set of simultaneous swaps is a maximum-weight matching on that complement graph.
  4. On 2×3 the complement already contains two triangles, so it is not bipartite.
  5. Therefore Hungarian is inapplicable: it needs two distinct sides, and the odd-set polytope it cannot express would let a fractional answer of 1.5 stand in for a true optimum of 1.
  6. Blossom handles exactly the missing ingredient — odd sets — and returns the provably optimal matching (verified here against brute force over all 25 matchings).
  7. The matched pairs must additionally form an induced matching or the gains do not add up; measured directly, predicting 10 delivers only 6.
